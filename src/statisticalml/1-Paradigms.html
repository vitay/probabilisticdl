Learning paradigms

Machine learning is usually divided into supervised, unsupervised and self-supervised learning, according to the kind of data available. From the probabilistic point of view of this book, the distinction is simpler: all these paradigms learn a probability distribution, and they only differ by which distribution is learned. This chapter reviews the main paradigms from this point of view (Figure 6.1), before the next chapters show how they are all trained in the same way.

Discriminative models: p_\theta(y | x)

In supervised learning, the training data are pairs (x_i, y_i) of an input and a target, for example an image and the name of the object it represents. They are samples of an unknown joint distribution p(x, y). A discriminative model does not try to model the inputs: it learns the conditional distribution p(y | x) of the target given the input. A neural network takes x as input and outputs the parameters of a distribution p_\theta(y | x) over the possible targets (Figure 6.1, a).

The family of distributions depends on the nature of the targets:

  • If the targets are discrete, for example classes, the network outputs a categorical distribution through a softmax layer: this is classification (Softmax classifier for discrete distributions).
  • If the targets are continuous, the simplest choice is a normal distribution whose mean is predicted by the network, with a fixed variance: maximizing its likelihood is the same as minimizing the mean square error, which is classical regression. The network can also predict the variance, to estimate how uncertain each prediction is.
  • If the conditional distribution has several modes, as in the inverse problems of Introduction, a single normal distribution is not enough, and the network can output a mixture of normal distributions: this is a mixture density network (Mixture density networks for continuous distributions).

In all cases, the prediction is a distribution: the most probable class or the mean of the normal distribution are only summaries of it.

Generative models: p_\theta(x)

In unsupervised learning, there are no targets: the training data are only samples x_i of an unknown distribution p(x), for example a collection of images. A generative model learns this distribution itself, p_\theta(x) \approx p(x). Two different abilities are expected from such a model, which are not always provided by the same methods:

  • Density estimation consists in evaluating the likelihood p_\theta(x) of a sample (Figure 6.1, b). It tells how typical a sample is of the data: a new sample with a very low likelihood is unusual, which is the basis of anomaly detection.
  • Generation consists in sampling new data from p_\theta(x) (Figure 6.1, c). In most modern methods, a network transforms a sample z of a simple distribution, typically the standard normal distribution, into a sample x of the learned distribution. The decoder of a variational autoencoder (Variational autoencoders), diffusion models (Denoising diffusion models) and flow matching models (Flow matching) all work this way.

Some models provide both abilities, but many only provide one of them easily: diffusion models generate excellent samples, but computing the likelihood of a sample is expensive, while some density models are easy to evaluate but very difficult to sample. This distinction explains many of the design choices of the generative models of this book.

Autoregressive models

Sequences, such as texts, sounds or time series, can be modeled one element at a time. The chain rule of probabilities decomposes the distribution of a whole sequence x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T) into a product of conditional distributions, each element depending on all the previous ones:

p(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T) = p(x_1) \, p(x_2 | x_1) \, p(x_3 | x_1, x_2) \ldots = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t | x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})

An autoregressive model learns each of these conditional distributions with the same network, which predicts the next element given the previous ones (Figure 6.1, d). Each factor is a discriminative problem, a classification when the elements are discrete, but the product is a generative model of the whole sequence, which provides both abilities:

  • the likelihood of a sequence is the product of the predicted probabilities of its elements;
  • a new sequence is generated one element at a time, each one being sampled from the prediction and appended to the input of the next step (ancestral sampling, Sampling from simple distributions).

Large language models are autoregressive models of text: given the beginning of a text, a transformer outputs a softmax distribution over a vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens, and text is generated by sampling one token at a time. Autoregressive models also exist for images, predicting each pixel from the previous ones, and for sound.

As the targets of an autoregressive model are taken from the data themselves, without any human annotation, this paradigm is also called self-supervised learning. A related family of self-supervised methods, masked modeling, hides part of the data and trains a network to predict it from the rest, for example missing words in a sentence or missing patches in an image.

Summary

Paradigm Learned distribution Training data Examples
Discriminative (supervised) p_\theta(y \mid x) pairs (x_i, y_i) classification, regression, MDN
Generative (unsupervised) p_\theta(x) samples x_i VAE, diffusion models, flow matching
Autoregressive (self-supervised) \prod_t p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) sequences language models

Whatever the paradigm, learning consists in making a parameterized distribution p_\theta as close as possible to an unknown data distribution p, of which we only have samples. The next chapter shows that the natural measure of closeness, the KL divergence, can be minimized with samples only, and that minimizing it is the same as maximizing the likelihood of the data.
