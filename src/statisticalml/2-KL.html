Kullback-Leibler divergence in machine learning

Let us now look at maximum likelihood estimation / KL minimization from the information theory point of view.

The definition of the KL divergence (Kullback-Leibler divergence) relies on the mathematical expectation of the negative log-likelihood of the outcomes of p:

\text{KL}(p || q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log \dfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}]

There are basically two ways to compute the KL, one relying on closed analytical forms, the other on sampling. We focus here on the first one; sampling methods are the topic of Monte Carlo and importance sampling.

Closed form of the KL divergence for normal distributions

The first method is possible when both distributions are normal distributions. Let us suppose that p and q are both univariate normal distributions:

p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_p, \sigma_p) \qquad\qquad\qquad q(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_q, \sigma_q)

TipKL for multivariate normal distributions

For multivariate normal distributions, see https://mr-easy.github.io/2020-04-16-kl-divergence-between-2-gaussian-distributions/. The formula looks the same, but with vectors and matrices.

We can use the known pdf of normal distributions to get the KL:

\begin{aligned} \text{KL}(p || q) &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log \dfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log \dfrac{\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_q, \sigma_q)}{\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_p, \sigma_p)}] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[ \log \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_p, \sigma_p) - \log \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_q, \sigma_q)] \\ \end{aligned}

As:

\mathcal{N}(x ; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, \exp{- \dfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \, \sigma^2}}

the log-likelihood of a normal distribution is:

\begin{aligned} \log \mathcal{N}(x ; \mu, \sigma) &= \log [\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} \, \exp{- \dfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \, \sigma^2}}] \\ &= -\dfrac{1}{2} \log 2 \pi \sigma^2 - \dfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \, \sigma^2}\\ &= - \dfrac{1}{2} [ \log 2 \pi + 2 \, \log \sigma + \dfrac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2} ]\\ \end{aligned}

This gives for the KL:

\begin{aligned} \text{KL}(p || q) &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[ \log \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_p, \sigma_p) - \log \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_q, \sigma_q)] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[\dfrac{1}{2} \, [2 \, \log \sigma_q + \dfrac{(x - \mu_q)^2}{\sigma_q^2} - 2 \, \log \sigma_p - \dfrac{(x - \mu_p)^2}{\sigma_p^2}]]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [\log \dfrac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} + \dfrac{(x - \mu_q)^2}{2 \sigma_q^2} - \dfrac{(x - \mu_p)^2}{2 \sigma_p^2}]\\ &= \log \dfrac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} + \dfrac{1}{2 \sigma_q^2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_q)^2] - \dfrac{1}{2 \sigma_p^2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p)^2]\\ \end{aligned}

By definition of the variance, we have:

\sigma_p^2 = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p)^2]

Moreover, as \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [x - \mu_p] = 0:

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_q)^2] &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p + \mu_p - \mu_q)^2]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p)^2] + 2 \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p) \, (\mu_p - \mu_q)] + \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(\mu_p - \mu_q)^2] \\ &= \sigma_p^2 + (\mu_p - \mu_q)^2 \\ \end{aligned}

which gives:

\begin{aligned} \text{KL}(p || q) &= \log \dfrac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} + \dfrac{1}{2 \sigma_q^2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_q)^2] - \dfrac{1}{2 \sigma_p^2} \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [(x - \mu_p)^2]\\ &= \log \dfrac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} + \dfrac{\sigma_p^2 + (\mu_p - \mu_q)^2}{2 \sigma_q^2} - \dfrac{\sigma_p^2}{2 \sigma_p^2} \\ \end{aligned}

NoteClosed form of the KL divergence between two Gaussians

The closed form of the KL divergence between p = \mathcal{N}(\mu_p, \sigma_p) and q = \mathcal{N}(\mu_q, \sigma_q) is simply a function of their parameters:

\text{KL}(p || q) = \log \dfrac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} + \dfrac{\sigma_p^2 + (\mu_p - \mu_q)^2}{2 \sigma_q^2} - \dfrac{1}{2}

We can check that when \mu_p = \mu_q and \sigma_p = \sigma_q, the KL is zero.

When q is the standard normal distribution \mathcal{N}(0, 1), we have:

\text{KL}(p || \mathcal{N}(0, 1)) = \dfrac{1}{2} [\sigma_p^2 + \mu_p^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_p^2]

Equivalence between KL and cross-entropy

Let’s now consider that the samples X = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^N come from some unknown distribution p (the data), while Y comes from a parameterized family of probability distributions p_\theta(x) with parameters \theta (the model). The KL divergence is expressed as:

\begin{aligned} \text{KL}(p || p_\theta) & = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log \dfrac{p_\theta(x)}{p(x)}] \\ & = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log p_\theta(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log p(x)] \\ & = H(p(x), p_\theta(x)) - H(p(x)) \\ \end{aligned}

The KL between the data and the model is the difference between their cross-entropy and the entropy of the data.

If we want the model p_\theta to explain the data \{x_i\}_{i=1}^N sampled from p, we want the KL divergence between p_\theta and p to be zero, as both distributions would then be the same. In other terms, we search for a value of \theta where the KL divergence between the model and the data is minimal: the KL is always positive, so its minimum is 0.

\theta^* = \text{argmin}_\theta \, \text{KL}(p(x) || p_\theta(x))

Minimizing the KL with respect to \theta requires access to its gradient, i.e. the partial derivatives of the KL divergence with respect to each parameter. Gradient descent can for example be used to iteratively update the parameters in the opposite direction to this gradient:

\Delta \theta = - \eta \, \nabla_\theta \, \text{KL}(p(x) || p_\theta(x))

Let us have a look at this gradient:

\nabla_\theta \, \text{KL}(p(x) || p_\theta(x)) = \nabla_\theta \, H(p(x), p_\theta(x)) - \nabla_\theta \, H(p(x))

The gradient of the KL is the gradient of the cross-entropy minus the gradient of the entropy of the data. However, the data distribution p(x) does not depend on the parameters \theta of the model, so the gradient of the entropy of the data is zero:

\nabla_\theta \, H(p(x)) = 0

This means that the gradient of the KL is the same as the gradient of the cross-entropy:

\nabla_\theta \, \text{KL}(p(x) || p_\theta(x)) = \nabla_\theta \, H(p(x), p_\theta(x))

Minimizing the KL divergence between a model and the data is the same as minimizing their cross-entropy!

The KL divergence and the cross-entropy only differ by a constant term, the entropy of the data, which does not depend on the model. The value of \theta that minimizes one also minimizes the other.

Let us now estimate the cross-entropy:

H(p(x), p_\theta(x)) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log p_\theta(x)]

Using N samples \{x_i\}_{i=1}^N from the data, we can approximate this expectation with a Monte Carlo estimate (Monte Carlo and importance sampling), i.e. the average of - \log p_\theta(x) over the samples:

H(p(x), p_\theta(x)) \approx \dfrac{1}{N} \, \sum_{i=1}^N - \log p_\theta(x_i) = - \dfrac{1}{N} \, l(\theta)

where l(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_i) is the log-likelihood of the model on the data that we introduced in maximum likelihood estimation (Maximum likelihood estimation). Maximizing the log-likelihood minimizes the cross-entropy and therefore the KL divergence.

This means that MLE is strictly equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence (or cross-entropy) between the model p_\theta and the distribution of the data p!

\theta_\text{MLE} = \text{argmax}_\theta \, l(\theta) = \text{argmin}_\theta \, H(p(x), p_\theta(x)) = \text{argmin}_\theta \, \text{KL}(p(x) || p_\theta(x))

Any machine learning model with a probabilistic interpretation that tries to fit a parameterized model to some data is implicitly minimizing the KL divergence between the two distributions.

Depending on the assumption about the underlying distribution, we can use the following neural network architectures:

  • Discrete / categorical / multinoulli distribution: softmax classifier.
  • Continuous distribution: mixture density network (MDN, Bishop, 1994) or Gaussian Process regression (GPR, but it is a nonparametric kernel-based probabilistic model, not a neural network).

Forward vs. reverse KL

The KL divergence is not symmetric (Kullback-Leibler divergence): \text{KL}(p || q) and \text{KL}(q || p) are different quantities, and minimizing one or the other leads to very different results. Let’s call p the target distribution and q the approximation we fit to it. Maximum likelihood fits a model q = p_\theta to the distribution of the data p; variational inference (Variational inference) will fit an approximate posterior q = q_\phi(z | x) to a true posterior p = p_\theta(z | x).

Two directions

The forward KL is an expectation under the target:

\text{KL}(p || q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)}[- \log \dfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}]

Estimating it requires samples of the target. This is the situation of the previous section: we have data, i.e. samples of p, and minimizing the forward KL is maximum likelihood estimation.

The reverse KL is an expectation under the approximation:

\text{KL}(q || p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q(x)}[- \log \dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}]

Estimating it requires samples of the approximation, which we choose easy to sample, and the ability to evaluate the target, at least up to its normalization constant: if p(x) = \tilde{p}(x) / Z, then \log p(x) = \log \tilde{p}(x) - \log Z, and the constant \log Z does not change the minimum. This is the situation of Bayesian inference, where the posterior can be evaluated up to the evidence but not sampled: variational inference minimizes the reverse KL.

In practice, the choice of the direction is therefore usually imposed by what we have: samples of the target, or its unnormalized density.

Where is the penalty?

The two directions do not penalize the same errors:

  • In the forward KL, the log-ratio is weighted by the target p(x). The penalty is very large where p(x) > 0 but q(x) \approx 0, as - \log q(x) explodes, while regions where p(x) \approx 0 do not count at all. The approximation must put mass everywhere the target has mass, even if this means putting mass where the target has none: the forward KL is mass-covering.
  • In the reverse KL, the log-ratio is weighted by the approximation q(x). The penalty is very large where q(x) > 0 but p(x) \approx 0, while regions where q(x) \approx 0 do not count at all. The approximation must avoid the regions where the target is small, even if this means ignoring whole parts of the target: the reverse KL is mode-seeking.

Figure 7.1 illustrates the difference by fitting a single normal distribution q(x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma) to a bimodal target. With the forward KL, the best normal distribution covers both modes: it is broad, and puts most of its mass in the valley between the modes, where the target has almost none. For a normal approximation, the optimum of the forward KL simply matches the mean and the variance of the target. With the reverse KL, the normal distribution settles on one of the modes and ignores the other: there are two local optima, and which one is found depends on the initialization of the parameters.

The behavior of the reverse KL depends on how separated the modes are. If they are close to each other, as in the bimodal target of Monte Carlo and importance sampling, covering both modes with a single broad distribution costs less than ignoring one of them, and the reverse KL also prefers a broad fit; the two directions differ more and more when the modes move apart.

Consequences

These two behaviors have practical consequences throughout this book:

  • Maximum likelihood (forward KL) produces models that try to cover all the data. When the model is not flexible enough, it spreads probability between the modes of the data, and generates “averaged” samples that do not look like any real one, which is one reason for the blurry samples of variational autoencoders (Variational autoencoders).
  • Variational inference (reverse KL) produces approximate posteriors that are too confident: they underestimate the uncertainty, and can miss whole modes of the posterior. This is the same failure as a too narrow proposal in importance sampling (Importance sampling) or an MCMC chain stuck in one mode (Using MCMC in practice), and an important limitation for the multimodal posteriors of inverse problems (Inverse problems).

Other divergences interpolate between these two behaviors or are symmetric, for example the Jensen-Shannon divergence, which is at the heart of generative adversarial networks.
