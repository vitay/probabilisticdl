Latent variable models

In generative modeling (Learning paradigms), the data X = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^N are samples of an unknown distribution p(x), for example the distribution of natural images, and we want to learn a parameterized distribution p_\theta(x) as close as possible to it, in order to generate new samples. As seen in Kullback-Leibler divergence in machine learning, minimizing the KL divergence between p(x) and p_\theta(x) is the same as maximizing the log-likelihood of the model on the data:

\max_\theta \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [\log p_\theta(x)] \approx \max_\theta \, \dfrac{1}{N} \, \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_i)

For images, this is much harder than it looks, because of the dimension of the data. This chapter introduces the main idea used to tame it: describing the data with a few hidden variables.

Images live on a manifold

A grayscale image of 32 \times 32 pixels is a point in a space with 1024 dimensions, one per pixel. This space is huge, and almost all of it is noise: if we pick a point at random, i.e. a random value for each pixel, we obtain an image of static, never a face or a digit. Natural images occupy a tiny fraction of the pixel space, which is why their distribution is so hard to model directly (Numerical integration does not scale).

These images are not scattered randomly in the pixel space either: they are organized. Let’s take a simple example: images of a digit “7”, which only differ by their rotation and their size (Figure 12.1). Each image is a point in the 1024-dimensional pixel space, but as only two factors vary, two numbers are enough to describe it. When the rotation or the size changes a little, the image changes a little: the images form a continuous 2D surface inside the pixel space. The left part of Figure 12.1 shows this surface, projected on the three directions of the pixel space along which the images vary the most: it is curved and folded, but it is a surface, and each of its points is an image.

Such a surface is called a manifold: a space that looks flat when seen up close, but can be curved globally. The surface of the earth is the classical example: it is locally flat and two-dimensional (a map of a city works fine), but globally a sphere in three dimensions. The right part of Figure 12.1 shows the same images on a flat 2D map, where each image is described by its two coordinates: its rotation and its size.

Natural images vary along many more factors than our “7”: the identity of a person, their pose, their expression, the lighting, the background… But these factors are still far fewer than the number of pixels. The manifold hypothesis states that natural data lie close to a manifold of much lower dimension than the space they live in. Learning the distribution of the data then comes down to learning where the data are on this low-dimensional manifold, which is a much easier problem.

Latent variables

The coordinates of the data on the manifold are called latent variables, and noted z. They are the actual causes of the observations: in our example, choosing a rotation and a size determines the image. A latent variable model describes how the data are generated in two steps:

  1. sample the latent variables from a simple distribution, the prior p_\theta(z), in the low-dimensional latent space;
  2. sample the observation from the decoder p_\theta(x | z), typically a neural network, which maps a point of the latent space to a point of the data space.

Generating new data is therefore easy: this is ancestral sampling (Sampling from simple distributions). The decoder plays the role of the map from the right part of Figure 12.1 to its left part.

There is one major difference with our toy example: for real data, the latent variables are not given. Nobody labels the images with their pose or their lighting, and the factors of variation are not even known in advance. The latent variables are hidden: we only observe x, and the latent space has to be learned together with the decoder. The learned latent variables do not need to be interpretable; they just need to explain the data.

Inverse problems

The decoder goes from the causes to the observations: given latent variables z, it produces an image x. This is the forward model, and it is easy to evaluate: we just run the network. Going the other way, from an observation x to the latent variables z that produced it, is an inverse problem. Inverse problems are usually much harder than the corresponding forward problems, because several causes can explain the same observation. We already met this situation in Mixture density networks for continuous distributions (Figure 8.3): a forward model can be a simple function, while its inverse has several solutions.

The answer to an inverse problem is therefore not a single value, but a distribution over all the possible causes, which can be multimodal: the posterior p_\theta(z | x). In a latent variable model, this posterior is the encoder.

Inverse problems are everywhere in science and engineering. We often observe a degraded version y of the signal x we are interested in:

y = A(x) + \text{noise}

where A describes the measurement process: noise (denoising), missing pixels (inpainting), a lower resolution (super-resolution), a blur, the projections of a CT scanner, or the sparse measurements of weather stations from which the state of the atmosphere must be reconstructed (data assimilation). Recovering x from y is ill-posed: there are infinitely many signals consistent with the observation. If pixels are missing, any value of these pixels explains the observed ones equally well.

Bayes’ rule tells us how to combine the observation with prior knowledge about the signal:

p(x | y) \propto p(y | x) \, p(x)

The likelihood p(y | x) measures how well a candidate signal x explains the observation, using the known measurement process A and the noise. The prior p(x) says which signals are plausible in the first place. This is where generative models come in: a latent variable model is a prior over the data, which restricts the candidates to the manifold of plausible signals. With x generated from z by the decoder, the inverse problem becomes a much smaller one, over the latent variables:

p(z | y) \propto p(y | z) \, p_\theta(z)

Figure 12.3 shows this on the images of Figure 12.1. Only a strip of three rows through the middle of an image is observed, with noise. In the pixel space, the 928 unobserved pixels could take any value. On the manifold, only two unknowns remain, the rotation and the size, and their posterior is concentrated on a narrow curve: all the combinations whose diagonal stroke crosses the strip at the observed position. Each sample of the posterior is a plausible restoration: they all look like a “7”, differ in their size and rotation, and the true image is one of them.

The posterior does not only give a restoration, it also tells us how uncertain it is: here, the observation constrains the position of the stroke, but not the size of the digit. A point estimate such as the MAP (Maximum a posteriori (MAP)) would return a single image and hide this uncertainty. Modern generative models, in particular diffusion models, are now used as priors to solve inverse problems in this way, for example in medical imaging (Song et al., 2022) or data assimilation (Rozet and Louppe, 2023).

The marginal likelihood is intractable

To train a latent variable model by maximum likelihood, we need the likelihood p_\theta(x) of each observation. The model only defines it indirectly: an image x can be produced by many different latent variables z, so its probability is obtained by summing over all of them (marginalization):

p_\theta(x) = \int_z p_\theta(x | z) \, p_\theta(z) \, dz = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_\theta(z)} [p_\theta(x | z)]

This integral over the whole latent space has no closed form when the decoder is a neural network. As it is an expectation, we could estimate it with Monte Carlo (The Monte Carlo estimator): sample latent variables from the prior, and average p_\theta(x | z). But for a given image x, almost all the latent variables sampled from the prior generate completely different images, for which p_\theta(x | z) is essentially zero: the estimate is dominated by the rare samples that happen to land in the right region, and its variance is enormous. This is the same problem as importance sampling with a bad proposal (Importance sampling).

To estimate p_\theta(x) efficiently, we would need to sample the latent variables that are likely to have generated x, i.e. to sample from the posterior p_\theta(z | x): we are back to the inverse problem of Inverse problems. In Figure 12.3, we could compute the posterior on a grid, as there were only two latent variables; with tens or hundreds of latent variables, no grid is possible (Numerical integration does not scale). Bayes’ rule gives:

p_\theta(z | x) = \dfrac{p_\theta(x | z) \, p_\theta(z)}{p_\theta(x)}

so computing the posterior requires the evidence p_\theta(x), which is the intractable integral we started from. Variational inference, presented in the next chapter, breaks this circle by approximating the posterior with a learned encoder.
