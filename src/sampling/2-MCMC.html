Markov chain Monte Carlo

The previous chapter ended on the limits of rejection and importance sampling: a proposal fixed once and for all cannot match a complex target in high dimensions. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods build samples one after the other, each new sample being proposed in the neighborhood of the current one. To understand why such a sequence of samples ends up following the target distribution, we first need a few notions about Markov chains.

Markov chains

Definition

A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots in which each state only depends on the previous one, not on the rest of the history:

p(x_{t+1} | x_t, x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_0) = p(x_{t+1} | x_t)

This is the Markov property: the chain is “memoryless”, the current state contains everything needed to predict the next one. The probability of moving from a state x to a state x' in one step is given by the transition kernel T(x' | x). We only consider chains where the transition kernel does not change over time.

Simulating a Markov chain is easy: start from an initial state x_0, then repeatedly sample the next state from the transition kernel, x_{t+1} \sim T(x' | x_t). When the states are discrete, this is just sampling from a categorical distribution (Sampling from simple distributions).

A classical toy example is the weather, with three possible states: sunny, cloudy and rainy. Each day, the weather of the next day is drawn according to the following transition probabilities (each row sums to one):

Today \backslash tomorrow sunny cloudy rainy
sunny 0.7 0.2 0.1
cloudy 0.3 0.4 0.3
rainy 0.2 0.4 0.4

A sunny day is followed by another sunny day with probability 0.7, by a cloudy day with probability 0.2, and by a rainy day with probability 0.1. The left part of Figure 11.1 represents the same chain as a graph.

Evolution of the distribution

If we know the state today, the state tomorrow is random. More generally, let’s call p_t(x) the probability that the chain is in state x at step t, i.e. the distribution we would observe over many independent runs of the chain. To be in state x' at step t+1, the chain must have been in some state x at step t and then moved from x to x'. Summing over all possible states x (law of total probability):

p_{t+1}(x') = \sum_x T(x' | x) \, p_t(x)

In the continuous case, the sum becomes an integral:

p_{t+1}(x') = \int_x T(x' | x) \, p_t(x) \, dx

Let’s start on a sunny day: p_0 = (1, 0, 0) for (sunny, cloudy, rainy). The next day, the distribution is given by the first row of the table: p_1 = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1). Applying the formula once more:

p_2(\text{sunny}) = 0.7 \times 0.7 + 0.3 \times 0.2 + 0.2 \times 0.1 = 0.57

and similarly for the two other states, giving p_2 = (0.57, 0.26, 0.17). Repeating this, we get p_3 = (0.51, 0.29, 0.20), p_4 = (0.48, 0.30, 0.22), p_5 = (0.47, 0.30, 0.22)… The distribution changes less and less: the chain progressively forgets that it started on a sunny day.

Stationary distribution

The middle part of Figure 11.1 shows that the distribution converges towards fixed values, around (0.46, 0.31, 0.23), and that these values are the same if we start on a rainy day instead. Once the chain has reached this distribution, applying the transition kernel does not change it anymore. Such a distribution p(x) is called a stationary distribution of the chain:

p(x') = \sum_x T(x' | x) \, p(x)

For the weather chain, we can check that p = (\frac{6}{13}, \frac{4}{13}, \frac{3}{13}) \approx (0.46, 0.31, 0.23) is stationary. For example, for the sunny state:

0.7 \times \dfrac{6}{13} + 0.3 \times \dfrac{4}{13} + 0.2 \times \dfrac{3}{13} = \dfrac{4.2 + 1.2 + 0.6}{13} = \dfrac{6}{13}

In the long run, it is sunny 46% of the days, cloudy 31% and rainy 23%, whatever the weather on the first day.

This convergence is not specific to this example. If two mild conditions are satisfied:

  • every state can be reached from every other state in a finite number of steps (the chain is irreducible),
  • the chain does not cycle deterministically between groups of states (the chain is aperiodic),

then the chain has a unique stationary distribution, and p_t converges to it from any initial state. We will not prove this result here; see for example Bishop (2006) or MacKay (2003).

Time averages

The middle part of Figure 11.1 describes many independent runs of the chain. What happens with a single run? The right part of the figure shows the fraction of the days spent in each state by one long chain: it also converges to the stationary distribution. A single chain that runs long enough visits each state a fraction of the time equal to its stationary probability.

More generally, the average of any function f over the states visited by the chain converges to its expectation under the stationary distribution:

\dfrac{1}{N} \, \sum_{t=1}^N f(x_t) \rightarrow \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x)} [f(x)] \qquad \text{when } N \rightarrow \infty

This is exactly the Monte Carlo estimator of The Monte Carlo estimator, except that the samples x_t are now the successive states of a chain instead of independent samples. The estimate still converges, but more slowly, as successive states are correlated: today’s weather tells us a lot about tomorrow’s.

Turning the problem around

In the usual study of Markov chains, the transition kernel T is given (the physics of the weather) and we want to find the stationary distribution. MCMC turns the problem around:

  • the target distribution p(x) = \tilde{p}(x) / Z is given, at least up to its normalization constant;
  • we design a transition kernel T whose stationary distribution is p(x), and which is easy to simulate.

We then simulate the chain for a long time, and use its successive states as (correlated) samples of p(x). The next section explains how to build such a transition kernel.

Detailed balance

Probability flows

To design a transition kernel T with a given stationary distribution p(x), we could try to satisfy the stationarity condition directly:

p(x') = \sum_x T(x' | x) \, p(x) \qquad \text{for all } x'

This condition is easier to interpret in terms of flows of probability. The term p(x) \, T(x' | x) is the probability that, at stationarity, the chain is in state x and moves to x' at the next step: it is the flow of probability going from x to x'. As the chain must go somewhere after x', we have \sum_x T(x | x') = 1, so the left-hand side can be written p(x') = \sum_x p(x') \, T(x | x'), and stationarity becomes:

\underbrace{\sum_x p(x) \, T(x' | x)}_{\text{flow into } x'} = \underbrace{\sum_x p(x') \, T(x | x')}_{\text{flow out of } x'} \qquad \text{for all } x'

For every state, the total flow coming in must equal the total flow going out. This is sometimes called global balance. Think of a crowd of people moving between the rooms of a building: the number of people in each room stays constant as long as, for every room, as many people enter as leave. Where they come from and where they go does not matter. People could, for example, all walk around the building in a loop A → B → C → A, without anybody ever walking back: the occupancy of the rooms would still be stationary.

Designing T with this condition is difficult: for every state x', it involves a sum (or an integral) over all the states of the space, which is exactly the kind of computation we want to avoid.

A local condition

Fortunately, there is a stronger condition that is much simpler to satisfy. A transition kernel satisfies detailed balance with respect to p(x) if, for every pair of states x and x':

p(x) \, T(x' | x) = p(x') \, T(x | x')

Instead of balancing the total flows of each state, detailed balance requires that the flows are balanced for every pair of states: the flow from x to x' equals the flow from x' to x. In the analogy of the crowd, between every pair of rooms, as many people go one way as the other. This condition only involves two states at a time: there is no sum over the whole space anymore.

Detailed balance implies global balance, hence stationarity: summing both sides over x gives exactly the flow into x' on the left and the flow out of x' on the right:

\sum_x p(x) \, T(x' | x) = \sum_x p(x') \, T(x | x') = p(x') \, \sum_x T(x | x') = p(x')

The converse is not true. In the loop A → B → C → A, the flow from A to B is positive, but there is no flow from B to A: detailed balance is violated, although the distribution is stationary. More generally, detailed balance requires every move to be possible in both directions: if T(x' | x) > 0, then T(x | x') > 0. Such chains are called reversible: at stationarity, a film of the chain looks statistically the same when played backwards, whereas a chain circulating in a loop has a preferred direction.

The weather chain of Markov chains satisfies detailed balance with respect to its stationary distribution p = (\frac{6}{13}, \frac{4}{13}, \frac{3}{13}). For each pair of states, the two flows are equal:

Pair of states flow one way flow the other way
sunny, cloudy \frac{6}{13} \times 0.2 = \frac{1.2}{13} \frac{4}{13} \times 0.3 = \frac{1.2}{13}
sunny, rainy \frac{6}{13} \times 0.1 = \frac{0.6}{13} \frac{3}{13} \times 0.2 = \frac{0.6}{13}
cloudy, rainy \frac{4}{13} \times 0.3 = \frac{1.2}{13} \frac{3}{13} \times 0.4 = \frac{1.2}{13}

Sufficient, but not necessary

Detailed balance is therefore a sufficient condition for stationarity, not a necessary one. The loop A → B → C → A is an extreme case (the chain is moreover periodic, so it does not converge), but the same happens with less extreme chains. Consider three states placed on a circle, and a chain that moves clockwise with probability 0.6 and counter-clockwise with probability 0.4. By symmetry, the uniform distribution p = (\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}) is stationary: each state receives as much probability as it gives away. But between two neighboring states, the clockwise flow \frac{1}{3} \times 0.6 is larger than the counter-clockwise flow \frac{1}{3} \times 0.4: probability keeps circulating around the circle, while the distribution stays the same.

For MCMC, we do not need to characterize all the chains that have p(x) as stationary distribution: it is enough to build one of them. Detailed balance is the design principle of most MCMC methods.

Why detailed balance suits MCMC

Detailed balance can be rewritten as a condition on the ratio of the transitions in both directions:

\dfrac{T(x' | x)}{T(x | x')} = \dfrac{p(x')}{p(x)} = \dfrac{\tilde{p}(x')}{\tilde{p}(x)}

The last equality is the key: the ratio of the probabilities of two states does not depend on the normalization constant Z, which cancels out. To build a transition kernel satisfying detailed balance, we only need to evaluate the unnormalized target \tilde{p}(x), at two points at a time. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, presented in the next section, does exactly that.

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm

The algorithm

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm (Hastings, 1970; Metropolis et al., 1953) builds a Markov chain satisfying detailed balance in two steps: a proposal step, which suggests a new state, and an acceptance step, which corrects the proposal so that the chain has the right stationary distribution.

The proposal distribution q(x' | x) plays the same role as in rejection and importance sampling (Rejection sampling), except that it now depends on the current state x: it proposes a new state in the neighborhood of the current one. A typical choice is a normal distribution centered on the current state, q(x' | x) = \mathcal{N}(x, s), where the standard deviation s is the step size. Starting from an initial state x_0, the algorithm repeats the following steps:

  1. sample a candidate x' \sim q(x' | x_t),
  2. compute the acceptance probability:

\alpha(x_t, x') = \min \left(1, \dfrac{\tilde{p}(x') \, q(x_t | x')}{\tilde{p}(x_t) \, q(x' | x_t)} \right)

  1. sample u \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1): if u \leq \alpha(x_t, x'), accept the candidate, x_{t+1} = x'; otherwise reject it and stay where we are, x_{t+1} = x_t.

The successive states x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots are (correlated) samples of p(x), once the chain has converged to its stationary distribution.

As announced in Detailed balance, the target only appears through the ratio \tilde{p}(x') / \tilde{p}(x_t): the normalization constant Z cancels out, and we only need to evaluate the unnormalized target \tilde{p}(x). This is exactly the situation of the posterior distributions of Sampling from simple distributions, whose numerator (likelihood times prior) is easy to evaluate but whose evidence is not.

The Metropolis rule

The ratio q(x_t | x') / q(x' | x_t) corrects for proposals that are asymmetric, i.e. that propose some moves more easily than the reverse moves. For the most common proposals, it simply disappears. A normal distribution centered on the current state is symmetric: the probability of proposing x' from x is the same as the probability of proposing x from x', as both only depend on the distance |x' - x|:

q(x' | x) = \mathcal{N}(x' ; x, s) = \mathcal{N}(x ; x', s) = q(x | x')

With such a symmetric proposal, the acceptance probability reduces to the original rule of Metropolis et al. (1953):

\alpha(x_t, x') = \min \left(1, \dfrac{\tilde{p}(x')}{\tilde{p}(x_t)} \right)

The algorithm is then called random-walk Metropolis, and its behavior is very intuitive:

  • if the candidate is more probable than the current state (\tilde{p}(x') \geq \tilde{p}(x_t)), it is always accepted: the chain always goes uphill;
  • if it is less probable, it is accepted with a probability equal to the ratio of the probabilities: a candidate half as probable as the current state is accepted half of the time, a candidate a thousand times less probable almost never.

Without the second rule, the chain would climb to the nearest mode and stay there, as an optimization algorithm would. Accepting some downhill moves is what allows the chain to explore the whole distribution, spending in each region a time proportional to its probability.

Rejected candidates are repeated

The acceptance step looks similar to rejection sampling (Rejection sampling), but there is an essential difference: when a candidate is rejected, the current state is repeated in the chain, x_{t+1} = x_t. It is not discarded, and the algorithm does not simply try again.

These repetitions are what gives each state its correct weight. A state of high probability is hard to leave: most candidates around it are less probable and get rejected, so the chain stays there for several steps, and the state appears several times in the chain. A state of low probability is left quickly. If we only kept the accepted candidates, the chain would spend as much “time” in the low-probability regions as in the high-probability ones, and its samples would not follow p(x).

NoteProof that Metropolis-Hastings satisfies detailed balance

We need to show that p(x) \, T(x' | x) = p(x') \, T(x | x') for every pair of states. When x' = x, both sides are equal, so let’s suppose x' \neq x. The only way to move from x to x' is to propose x' and to accept it, so the transition kernel is:

T(x' | x) = q(x' | x) \, \alpha(x, x')

The flow from x to x' is then:

\begin{aligned} p(x) \, T(x' | x) &= p(x) \, q(x' | x) \, \min \left(1, \dfrac{p(x') \, q(x | x')}{p(x) \, q(x' | x)} \right) & \text{(} Z \text{ cancels, so we can use } p \text{ instead of } \tilde{p} \text{)} \\ &= \min \left( p(x) \, q(x' | x), \; p(x') \, q(x | x') \right) & \text{(multiplying both terms of the min)} \\ \end{aligned}

This expression is symmetric in x and x': swapping the two states gives the same result. The flow from x' to x, p(x') \, T(x | x'), is therefore equal to the flow from x to x': detailed balance holds, and p(x) is a stationary distribution of the chain (Detailed balance).

The acceptance probability is in fact built for this: the minimum makes the flow from x to x' equal to the smaller of the two “proposal flows” p(x) \, q(x' | x) and p(x') \, q(x | x'). The direction with the larger proposal flow is slowed down by rejecting some of its moves, until both directions are balanced.

Example

Figure 11.2 applies random-walk Metropolis to the bimodal target of Figure 10.5, with the step size s = 1 and the initial state x_0 = 0. Only the unnormalized target \tilde{p}(x) (with Z = 3) is used.

The trace on the left shows the typical behavior of the chain:

  • it wanders around the first mode (x \approx -1.5) for about 140 steps, then moves to the second one (x \approx 1.5);
  • most rejected candidates fall in regions of low probability: in the tails, or in the valley between the modes;
  • rejections create flat segments, where the same state is repeated.

Over 10000 steps, 65% of the candidates are accepted, and the histogram of the chain matches the target (right). The average of the chain, -0.34, is close to the true mean \mathbb{E}_p[x] = -0.3.

Two things are visible in the trace, however. First, successive samples are strongly correlated: the chain moves by small steps, and stays in the same mode for many steps in a row (about 25 on average over the whole chain, 140 at the beginning of the trace). Second, the result depends on the step size s and on the initial state x_0. The next section discusses these practical aspects.

Using MCMC in practice

Metropolis-Hastings is guaranteed to produce samples of p(x) in the limit of infinitely long chains. With a finite chain, the quality of the estimates depends on choices that the algorithm leaves open: the step size of the proposal, the initial state, and the number of steps. This section discusses them on the bimodal target, with three different step sizes (Figure 11.3).

Choosing the step size

The step size s of the random-walk proposal q(x' | x) = \mathcal{N}(x, s) controls a trade-off between the size of the moves and their acceptance:

  • Too small (s = 0.1, top row): the candidates are so close to the current state that their probability is almost the same, and nearly all of them are accepted (96%). But each move is tiny: the chain behaves like a random walk with small steps and explores the distribution very slowly. After 10000 steps, it has spent most of its time in the minor mode on the right, and the histogram does not match the target.
  • Too large (s = 50, bottom row): the candidates land anywhere, mostly far in the tails where the target is negligible, and nearly all of them are rejected (3%). The chain stays still for tens of steps, and the few accepted moves are large jumps.
  • Good (s = 3, middle row): the candidates are far enough to explore the distribution quickly, and close enough to be accepted reasonably often (42%). The chain jumps between the two modes all the time, and its histogram matches the target.

A high acceptance rate is therefore not a sign of a good sampler: it is often the sign of a too small step size. The usual rule of thumb is to tune the step size so that the acceptance rate is between 25% and 50%. In high dimensions, the optimal acceptance rate of random-walk Metropolis approaches 23% (Roberts et al., 1997). In practice, the step size is adapted during the burn-in period (see below) until the acceptance rate falls into this range.

Note that the step size s = 1 used in Figure 11.2 was a bit too small: its acceptance rate is higher (65%), but it produces about four times fewer effective samples than s = 3 (in the sense defined below).

Burn-in

The chain starts from an arbitrary initial state x_0, often far from the regions of high probability: in Figure 11.3, all chains start at x_0 = 8, deep in the tail of the target. The first states of the chain depend on this initial state, not on the target: the distribution p_t of the chain has not converged to its stationary distribution yet (Markov chains).

These first samples are therefore discarded: this is the burn-in (or warm-up) period. Its required length depends on how fast the chain converges. With the good step size, the chain reaches the target in a couple of steps; with the too small one, it needs hundreds of steps just to walk down from x_0 = 8 to the region of high probability. In practice, the burn-in length is chosen by looking at the trace: the chain should have “forgotten” its initial state, i.e. fluctuate around a stable level. Discarding a few hundred or thousand steps is common.

Correlated samples

After burn-in, the samples of the chain follow the target, but they are not independent: each state is close to the previous one, or even equal to it when a candidate is rejected. Two successive samples therefore carry partly the same information, and a chain of N steps is worth less than N independent samples.

The right column of Figure 11.3 measures this dependence with the autocorrelation of the chain: how similar two states k steps apart are, on a scale from 1 (identical) to 0 (unrelated). With the good step size, the similarity fades within a few steps. With the too large step size, it takes almost a hundred steps, as the chain repeats the same state for tens of steps. With the too small one, two states 200 steps apart are still strongly related.

The effective sample size (ESS) of the chain summarizes this: it is the number of independent samples that would give an estimate of the same precision. It has the same meaning as the effective sample size of importance sampling (Importance sampling). In Figure 11.3, the 9500 samples kept after burn-in are worth about 1600 independent samples with the good step size, 190 with the too large one, and only 8 with the too small one. The ESS is the number to look at when comparing samplers: a sampler that is twice as slow per step but produces three times more effective samples is the better one.

NoteAutocorrelation and effective sample size

The autocorrelation of the chain at lag k is the correlation between the states x_t and x_{t+k}:

\rho_k = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(x_t, x_{t+k})}{\text{Var}(x_t)}

with \rho_0 = 1, and \rho_k = 0 for all k > 0 if the samples are independent. For N correlated samples, the variance \sigma_f^2 / N of the Monte Carlo estimator (The Monte Carlo estimator) becomes approximately (we admit this result):

\text{Var}(\hat{f}_N) \approx \dfrac{\sigma_f^2}{N} \, \left(1 + 2 \, \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k \right)

This is the variance that N / (1 + 2 \sum_k \rho_k) independent samples would give, hence the effective sample size:

\text{ESS} = \dfrac{N}{1 + 2 \, \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k}

In practice, the autocorrelations are estimated from the chain itself, and the sum is truncated when they become negligible (in Figure 11.3, at the first negative value).

Some implementations keep only one state every k steps, which is called thinning. It saves memory, but does not add any information: the thinned chain is not worth more independent samples than the full one.

Multimodality and multiple chains

The too small step size illustrates the most dangerous failure of MCMC. A chain that moves by small steps can stay in one mode for a very long time, as crossing the low-probability valley between two modes requires many unlikely moves in a row. Nothing in its trace reveals this: the chain fluctuates around a stable level and looks converged. This is the same “missed mode” problem as with a too narrow importance sampling proposal (Importance sampling).

The standard diagnostic is to run several chains from different initial states and to compare their estimates. If they disagree, at least some of them have not explored the whole target. With the too small step size, chains started on different sides of the target indeed give very different estimates of the mean, while with the good step size they all agree. Probabilistic programming libraries automate this comparison.

Agreement between chains is, however, not a proof of convergence: if all chains start in the same mode, they can all miss the other ones.

Gibbs sampling

One coordinate at a time

Metropolis-Hastings needs a proposal distribution, whose step size must be tuned. Gibbs sampling (Geman and Geman, 1984) avoids this choice in multidimensional problems, by updating the coordinates of x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_d) one at a time. Each coordinate x_k is sampled from its distribution given the current values of all the other coordinates, noted x_{-k}:

x_k \sim p(x_k | x_{-k})

This distribution is called the full conditional of x_k. A complete pass over the coordinates is called a sweep:

  1. sample x_1 \sim p(x_1 | x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_d),
  2. sample x_2 \sim p(x_2 | x_1, x_3, \ldots, x_d), using the new value of x_1,
  3. … and so on until x_d.

The state at the end of each sweep is a new sample of the chain. There is no acceptance step: every new value is kept.

Gibbs sampling is in fact a special case of Metropolis-Hastings, where the proposal changes only one coordinate, by sampling it from its full conditional. With this proposal, the acceptance probability is always exactly 1, which is why no candidate is ever rejected.

NoteProof that the acceptance probability is 1

Let’s consider the update of the coordinate x_k: the candidate x' is equal to x, except for its coordinate x'_k, which is sampled from q(x' | x) = p(x'_k | x_{-k}). As the other coordinates are unchanged, x'_{-k} = x_{-k}. Using p(x) = p(x_k | x_{-k}) \, p(x_{-k}), the ratio in the acceptance probability of Metropolis-Hastings is:

\dfrac{p(x') \, q(x | x')}{p(x) \, q(x' | x)} = \dfrac{p(x'_k | x_{-k}) \, p(x_{-k}) \; p(x_k | x_{-k})}{p(x_k | x_{-k}) \, p(x_{-k}) \; p(x'_k | x_{-k})} = 1

All terms cancel: the candidate is always accepted. As Metropolis-Hastings satisfies detailed balance (The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm), each coordinate update leaves p(x) stationary, and so does a whole sweep.

When is it useful?

Gibbs sampling only makes sense when the full conditionals are easy to sample from, which happens more often than one might think: the joint distribution p(x) can be very complex, while each coordinate taken separately, all others being fixed, follows a simple distribution. This is typically the case in models built from simple conditional distributions:

  • in Bayesian models with conjugate priors (Maximum a posteriori (MAP)), the conditional distribution of each parameter, given the data and the other parameters, is a known distribution;
  • in latent variable models (Latent variable models), we can alternate between sampling the latent variables given the parameters, and the parameters given the latent variables.

Gibbs sampling was originally introduced for image restoration (Geman and Geman, 1984), where each pixel only depends on its neighbors, and was for a long time the workhorse of Bayesian statistics.

Example: correlated coordinates

Let’s apply Gibbs sampling to a bivariate normal distribution, with zero mean, unit variances and correlation \rho. Its full conditionals are one-dimensional normal distributions (we admit this result):

p(x_1 | x_2) = \mathcal{N}(\rho \, x_2, \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}) \qquad p(x_2 | x_1) = \mathcal{N}(\rho \, x_1, \sqrt{1 - \rho^2})

Each update moves the chain parallel to one axis: horizontally when x_1 is updated, vertically when x_2 is. Figure 11.4 shows the path of the chain in two cases.

  • When the coordinates are independent (\rho = 0, left), each conditional is the full distribution of the coordinate: every sweep produces an independent sample, wherever the chain was before. The first sweep immediately brings the chain from its initial state to the center of the distribution.
  • When the coordinates are strongly correlated (\rho = 0.95, right), the conditionals are very narrow: once x_2 is fixed, x_1 can only change a little, and vice versa. The chain can only move along the diagonal of the distribution with small, staircase-like steps, and successive samples are strongly correlated.

This is the main limitation of Gibbs sampling: it explores the distribution efficiently only when the coordinates are weakly dependent. Strong dependencies, which are common in practice (for example between the parameters of a model), make it as slow as a random walk with a too small step size. Grouping strongly correlated coordinates and sampling them jointly (blocked Gibbs sampling) reduces the problem, when their joint conditional is still simple.

Example: sampling a posterior

Let’s go back to the Bayesian estimation of the parameters of a normal distribution (Maximum a posteriori (MAP)). To keep the math simple, the MAP example assumed that the standard deviation \sigma of the data was known, and chose a normal prior for the mean \mu, so that the posterior was a normal distribution. Let’s now estimate both parameters \theta = (\mu, \sigma) from N = 20 samples X = \{x_i\} of \mathcal{N}(3, 2), with priors that are not conjugate: a broad normal prior \mathcal{N}(0, 5) for \mu, and a broad half-normal prior for \sigma (a normal distribution \mathcal{N}(0, 5) restricted to positive values).

The posterior has no simple closed form, but its unnormalized version, the likelihood times the prior, is easy to evaluate for any (\mu, \sigma):

\tilde{p}(\mu, \sigma | X) = p(X | \mu, \sigma) \, p(\mu) \, p(\sigma) = \prod_{i=1}^N \mathcal{N}(x_i ; \mu, \sigma) \; \mathcal{N}(\mu ; 0, 5) \; p(\sigma)

This is all Metropolis-Hastings needs: the evidence p(X) is never computed. With only two parameters, we could still normalize the posterior numerically on a grid, but the same code would work with hundreds of parameters, where no grid is possible (Numerical integration does not scale).

We use random-walk Metropolis in the two-dimensional space of the parameters, with the proposal \mathcal{N}((\mu, \sigma), 0.7) (the same step size in both directions), during 20000 steps after a burn-in of 1000 steps. Candidates with a negative standard deviation have a probability of zero under the prior: they are simply always rejected.

One practical detail matters: the likelihood is a product of N densities, which quickly becomes too small to be represented by floating-point numbers. The acceptance test is therefore computed in the log domain, by comparing \log u with the difference \log \tilde{p}(\mu', \sigma' | X) - \log \tilde{p}(\mu, \sigma | X), where the log-likelihood is a sum instead of a product, as in Maximum likelihood estimation.

The left part of Figure 11.5 shows the samples of the chain. Instead of a single point estimate, we get the whole posterior distribution of the parameters, and in particular how uncertain they are: with 20 data points, the mean is somewhere between about 1.8 and 3.5, and the standard deviation between 1.3 and 2.6. Any other quantity is a simple Monte Carlo average over the chain: the posterior mean of a parameter is the average of its samples, the probability that \mu > 3 is the fraction of the samples where this is true, and so on.

The same holds for the posterior predictive distribution, which was out of reach in Maximum a posteriori (MAP). It is an expectation over the posterior:

p(x | X) = \int p(x | \mu, \sigma) \, p(\mu, \sigma | X) \, d\mu \, d\sigma = \mathbb{E}_{(\mu, \sigma) \sim p(\mu, \sigma | X)} [\mathcal{N}(x ; \mu, \sigma)]

and is therefore estimated by averaging the normal densities corresponding to all the samples (\mu_t, \sigma_t) of the chain:

p(x | X) \approx \dfrac{1}{T} \, \sum_{t=1}^T \mathcal{N}(x ; \mu_t, \sigma_t)

The right part of Figure 11.5 shows the result. Each sample of the chain is a plausible normal distribution for the data; their average is broader than each of them, as it also takes into account our uncertainty about the parameters. With more data, the posterior would become narrower, and the predictive distribution would get closer to a single normal distribution.

This example is small, but it contains the whole workflow of Bayesian inference with MCMC: write down the unnormalized posterior (likelihood times prior), sample it with a Markov chain, and compute any quantity of interest as an average over the chain.

Limits: towards gradient-based samplers

Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs sampling solve the main problem of the previous chapter: they do not need a global proposal matching the whole target, only local moves and the ability to evaluate \tilde{p}(x). Their weakness is that these local moves are blind: a random-walk proposal draws candidates in random directions, without using any information about the shape of the target, and the acceptance step only corrects them afterwards.

In a few dimensions, this is not a problem, as a reasonable fraction of the random directions leads to regions of high probability. In high dimensions, almost all random directions lead away from the small region where the probability mass is concentrated (Numerical integration does not scale): a large step is then almost certainly rejected. To keep a reasonable acceptance rate, the step size must shrink when the dimension increases, and the chain explores the target with smaller and smaller steps. As for a random walk, the distance travelled only grows with the square root of the number of steps, so the number of steps needed to explore the target grows with the dimension. Gibbs sampling has the same problem as soon as the coordinates are correlated (Gibbs sampling), and it additionally requires simple conditional distributions.

The solution is to propose moves that follow the shape of the target. The gradient of the log-density \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x) points, at each state, in the direction where the probability increases fastest. Like the ratio \tilde{p}(x') / \tilde{p}(x), it does not depend on the normalization constant, as \log Z is a constant whose gradient is zero:

\nabla_x \log p(x) = \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x) - \nabla_x \log Z = \nabla_x \log \tilde{p}(x)

Moving along the gradient, plus some noise to keep exploring, is the idea of Langevin dynamics; simulating the motion of a particle on the landscape of the log-density is the idea of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Both methods make large moves that are nevertheless accepted with a high probability, and scale much better with the dimension. They are presented in the next chapter. The gradient of the log-density, called the score, will also be at the heart of score-based diffusion models.
